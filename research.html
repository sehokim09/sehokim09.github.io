Ionospheric remote sensing

Ionospheric sensing with ship signals

Ships at sea continuously broadcast their position over the Automatic Identification System (AIS), a VHF radio signal. At these low frequencies the signals are highly sensitive to the ionosphere: Faraday rotation of their polarization and changes in their propagation reveal the electron content along the path. We use AIS broadcasts as signals of opportunity to measure perturbations in ionospheric electron density.

A key application is tsunami detection: a tsunami pushes the atmosphere above it into waves that travel up and disturb the ionosphere. Dense ship traffic could turn the open ocean into a large network for spotting those disturbances.

Land remote sensing

Root-zone soil moisture

Root-zone soil moisture controls crop growth, drought and the carbon cycle, but it lies below the depth most satellite sensors can see. Signals of opportunity reflectometry (SoOp-R) reuses existing communication and navigation satellite transmissions across many frequencies. Lower frequencies penetrate deeper into soil and vegetation, so combining several of them makes it possible to retrieve soil moisture profiles and vegetation water content together.

Our work spans RF instrument development and field testing, retrieval algorithms and sensitivity analysis, and constellation and mission design (the open-source SOCRATES simulator). Dr. Kim was also a member of the team for NASA's SNOOPI P-band CubeSat mission, led by Prof. James L. Garrison at Purdue University.

Space plasma radio science

Magnetic fields & plasma with radio links

When a linearly polarized radio wave passes through a magnetized plasma, its plane of polarization rotates. The amount of rotation depends on both the electron density and the magnetic field along the path, so if one is known, the other can be solved for.

We use radio links between ground stations and spacecraft to measure this rotation and probe magnetic fields and plasma, from Earth's ionosphere to Jupiter's magnetosphere. Next, we are extending the technique to satellite-to-satellite crosslinks, which can sample regions that ground-based links cannot reach.

Cryosphere · GNSS-Reflectometry

Sea ice

Navigation satellite signals reflected off the polar oceans carry information about sea ice. At low grazing angles the reflections stay coherent over smooth ice, while near-nadir reflectivity responds to the ice surface itself. As a postdoctoral fellow at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Dr. Kim led a study that combines grazing-angle and near-nadir GNSS-R reflectivity from the Spire commercial smallsat constellation to retrieve sea-ice thickness.
